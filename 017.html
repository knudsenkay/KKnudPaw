Grooming All Spaniels

Spaniels are not one coat -- they are several. The English Cocker wears a dense, feathered coat that mats quickly; the Cavalier King Charles has a single silky coat with lighter upkeep; the Field Spaniel sits somewhere in between; and the Springer needs its own balance of feathering and practicality. This guide covers the shared essentials and the breed-specific details that matter.

Coat Types at a Glance

Spaniel type Coat character Grooming load
English Cocker Dense, silky, flat coat with heavy feathering on ears, chest, belly, legs, and pants. Can become woolly after neutering[reference:9]. High. Daily brushing recommended; professional groom every 6–8 weeks[reference:10][reference:11].
Cavalier King Charles Single, medium-long silky coat with feathering on ears, legs, and tail; no heavy undercoat[reference:12]. Moderate. Daily 10-minute brush-out; professional groom every 4–6 weeks for pet trims[reference:13].
Field Spaniel Medium-length, glossy, flat or slightly wavy coat; moderate feathering. Moderate to high. Regular brushing and trimming; hand-stripping or thinning can maintain texture[reference:14].
English Springer Medium-length, flat or wavy coat with feathering on ears, legs, and belly; often clipped for working practicality. Moderate. Less demanding than Cocker; working types often kept in a short clip[reference:15].

Grooming Styles Explained

Hand Stripping

What it is
Removing dead topcoat by hand or with a stripping knife. It preserves the harsh, flat texture and rich colour of a correct spaniel coat and is the traditional show method[reference:16].
Best for
Show Cockers, working spaniels with correct wiry texture, and owners who want the coat to lie flat and glossy[reference:17].

Clipping (Comfort Clip)

What it is
Cutting the coat short with clippers. It is faster, lower-maintenance, and kinder for a dog that gets wet or muddy often[reference:18].
The trade-off
Clipping softens the coat over time, making it woollier and more prone to matting. It also dulls the colour compared with hand-stripping[reference:19].

Carding

What it is
Removing dead undercoat with a fine-toothed carding blade -- not the topcoat. It helps the silky guard hairs lie flatter and improves skin health[reference:20].
When to use
Especially useful on Cocker Spaniels and other sporting breeds, and after clipping to preserve coat colour and skin condition[reference:21].

Sanitary / Puppy Groom

Puppy groom
A short, positive first grooming session -- usually around 12–14 weeks -- that introduces the puppy to handling, the bath, and the dryer without doing a full style[reference:22].
Sanitary trim
Tidying the hygiene areas (under the tail, belly, and paw pads) for cleanliness and comfort. Often included in a full groom.

Grooming Essentials

Brushing

Frequency
Cockers: daily brushing is recommended due to coat length[reference:23]. Cavaliers: a daily 10-minute brush-out is ideal[reference:24]. Other spaniels: at least every other day.
Tools & technique
Use a slicker brush for body and feathering, a pin brush for silky coats, and a metal comb to check down to the skin. Brush in lines, not just over the surface, or mats hide close to the skin.

Bathing

Schedule
Every 4–6 weeks for most spaniels. Cavaliers can be bathed monthly; Cockers every 4–6 weeks[reference:26].
Drying
Dry thoroughly, especially the ear feathering. Damp feathering kinks and curls as it dries, and trapped moisture under the ear flap invites infection[reference:27]. A drying coat helps the coat lie flat[reference:28].

Ear Care

Weekly checks
All spaniels have long, heavy, hairy ears that hang over the canal and trap warmth and moisture[reference:29]. Check weekly for smell, redness, or discharge -- these are vet signs, not grooming signs[reference:30].
Ventilation
Trim or pluck the hair under the ear flap and around the opening to let air in. Never push cotton buds deep into the canal.
After water
Dry the ears thoroughly after bathing, swimming, or wet walks.

Eye Care

Daily wipe
Cavaliers are especially prone to tear staining; wipe under the eyes daily with a damp soft cloth or sterile saline[reference:32].
Hair trimming
Keep the hair immediately around the eyes lightly trimmed to stop tears wicking into the coat.

Nail Trimming

Frequency
Every 3–4 weeks. If you hear the nails clicking on the floor, they are overdue[reference:33].

Dental Care

Daily brushing
Brush the teeth daily with a canine toothbrush and toothpaste. Small spaniels are prone to dental disease, and home care makes a real difference.

Feet & Grass Seeds

Pad tufts
Spaniel feet grow tufts between the pads that pick up mud, ice balls, and grass seeds. Keep the hair between the pads trimmed for traction and hygiene[reference:34].
Grass awns
From late spring to autumn, grass awns can work into the skin between the toes or into the ear. A swollen foot or a head-shaking dog after a walk is a vet job[reference:35].

Additional Tips

Neutered coats change

Many spaniels, especially after neutering, grow a thicker, softer, woollier coat -- sometimes called a cotton or puppy coat -- that tangles more easily and does not lie flat. This is a coat change, not a grooming failure. Carding, thinning, or a shorter clip can manage it[reference:36].

Never brush a dry tangle

Spray a light leave-in detangling conditioner into the coat before working through a knot. Brushing a dry tangle snaps the hair shaft and makes the mat worse.

Acclimatise early

Slowly get your spaniel used to having the face, mouth, ears, and feet handled -- and to the sound of the dryer -- before you need a full groom. Cooperative care makes every session easier[reference:37].

By matching the routine to the coat -- daily brushing for a Cocker, a lighter but still regular routine for a Cavalier, and the right choice between hand-stripping and clipping -- you can keep any spaniel comfortable, healthy, and looking the way the breed intended.
